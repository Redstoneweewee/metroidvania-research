Metroidvania Research

Seven game ideas, what makes them worth exploring, and the research behind them.

ECE 184 · Data: 16 October 2026 · Updated: 17 October 2026 · About a 9-minute read

The main idea is a deep-sea metroidvania built around movement and heat, where the diver floats upward and must push to go deeper. It keeps the diver, four underwater levels and molten dragon from the original pitch. It ranks /7 in the existing model comparison. The research helps compare ideas, although it cannot predict a new game's sales.

1. The lead idea: a deep-sea heat metroidvania

Descend through sunken kingdoms, drawing power from the same heat that threatens your life.

A diver explores four underwater levels, moving through the remains of a drowned kingdom toward a molten dragon. Heat powers the suit and its lure, so the vents that help you see and fight can also burn you. Because heat rises, the diver floats upward unless their boots grip a floor or their hands grip a ceiling handhold, so every step deeper is a choice. Enemies attack each other as well as the player; much of combat comes from leading them into each other's paths.

How it plays

The basic loop is to explore, use movement to fend off enemies, refill at vents and rest at diving bells. New abilities let you return to earlier areas and reach places you could not get to before. Heat does not drain over time; active abilities spend it. Deeper water is harder to see through with the same amount of heat. The diver naturally floats up, and only a downward kick or a vent takes you deeper.

For example, you could lead a pursuing creature toward another enemy, then kick or dash away while they fight. Your heat attacks help fend them off, while a longer float deals more damage if something is above you. A nearby vent can refill your heat and launch you toward the next passage.

Floating upward

The diver floats upward unless holding on. Specialized boots grip any floor you land on, so you can walk the seabed. Your hands grab handholds on ceilings, such as chains, pipes, ship ribs and roots, and you can crawl along them at walking speed. Kick off a floor or ceiling, or step past an edge, and you start to move freely again. Going deeper takes effort, while going back up is free.

You can only hold onto what pushes back. The boots clamp you to floors, and your own buoyancy presses you up into ceilings, so your hands only have to stop you sliding. Nothing presses you into a side wall, so touching one makes you slide upward along it. Bare stone ceilings offer nothing to grab, which keeps handholds to the places a level designer puts them.

The reason is the heat itself. Warm things rise and cold things sink: the drowned kingdoms sank when they lost their heat, and a diver carrying heat has to push against the sea to follow them.

Passive and active movement

Passive moves take you upward without spending heat; active moves spend heat to propel you. Drift happens naturally when you are not grounded or moving. Float needs an input, so “passive” describes the heat cost.

TypeMoveWhat it does
PassiveDriftRise slowly while steering, with a small heat aura around the suit. It uses no heat.
PassiveFloatFloat straight up quickly and leave a vertical heat trail. It uses no heat, and deals more damage the farther you float.
ActiveKickGive yourself a short downward impulse, with a burst of heat just above your head. It has a low heat cost and very little attack range. Along with vents, it is the only way to go deeper. Kicking off a floor or ceiling pushes you away from it with a stronger initial boost.
ActiveDashDash left or right and leave a horizontal heat trail. It spends more heat than a kick.

Heat trails last about 5–10 seconds. Overlapping trails refresh a capped burn rather than multiplying damage. Your own heat does not hurt you.

Proposed keyboard controls: WASD aims vent rebounds, A/D steers horizontally, Space kicks downward, Shift with A/D dashes, and holding W floats. Kicking or floating while grounded lifts you off the floor. Floating into a handhold grabs it automatically, A/D crawls along it, and Space pushes off downward with a boost. When you stop floating and are not grounded, you return to a slow upward drift.

Enemies fighting each other

Your movement attacks still deal damage, but mainly help you fend off creatures while you lead them toward one another. Getting enemies to fight is the main way to kill them, so where you move changes which creatures meet. A room with multiple enemies becomes something you can use, rather than just more things attacking you. Handholds also let you hang above a room and wait for creatures to fight before kicking down.

Possible addition: some enemies could fear heat and move away from an aura or trail. This is still an idea.

Pull, bounce, push

Hot vents pull you inward, like Minecraft's magma blocks. One circular glow shows where you can refill heat, while a differently coloured glow shows where kick or dash can launch you outward. Light points in the direction you will go. Moving closer lets you build more speed, but leaves less time to react. Touching the core burns you and pushes you back slightly.

Cool current generators push you outward, like Minecraft's soul sand. Walls and passages channel the flow into routes through the room. They do not refill heat, although your movement attacks still work while riding them.

You refuel when you enter the vent's refueling glow. A vent can propel you even at zero heat, and its outward push briefly takes priority over the inward pull so it does not immediately drag you back.

Because you float, a vent below you is the main way to cover a long drop: its pull draws you down, and a launch from its glow can carry you farther. Rising currents add to your float, so they mostly block routes downward.

Recovery and exploration

Heat and health each run from 0 to 100. Diving bells hang from above, so you float up into them; they restore both, save progress and reveal the nearby map. At zero heat, kicks and dashes still work but become weaker; your moves stop producing heat damage, trails and the drift aura, and you can barely see. A zero-heat kick only slows your rise, so running dry sends you back up toward the last vent or bell instead of leaving you stuck deep. The movement penalty is binary: Any amount of heat above zero gives normal movement strength, so getting low does not gradually slow you down. Required routes need a reachable refill or return path, and health damage does not reduce movement reach.

What opens the map

Pressure valves are world objects. After a regional objective or boss, turn a valve to equalize a pressure lock and permanently open a route. The following reusable abilities open both the main path and secrets in earlier areas:

  • Current riding: the reward from the boss in Level 1. Hold and steer within outward flows, including sinking channels that carry you deeper, to reach Level 2 and earlier alcoves.
  • Current breaker: found in Level 2. A charged dash or kick crosses opposing currents, including rising currents that block the way down, and breaks fragile coral, opening routes you could not reach otherwise.
  • Lure flare: the reward from the boss in Level 2. Spend heat to briefly reveal routes, items and clues, including the next valve's path. Required clues have a nearby refill.
  • Heat sink: the reward from the boss in Level 3. Brief protection from environmental heat, followed by recovery, opens Level 4 and overheated side paths. Enemy bites still hurt.

Dashes stay horizontal, kicks give a short drop, and vents provide the longer descents. This limits how deep the starting moves can reach, although horizontal dashes alone do not solve every access problem: The depth gained by repeated kicks still needs testing. Rising never needs an ability, so returning upward is always free. Secret routes should become reachable through what an unlocked ability lets you do, rather than a door that simply checks whether you own it.

The optional harpoon tether remains outside the initial scope. If added, its swing would leave a short heated wake.

The world and story

From the pier, descend through four levels. Vents become scarce until Level 4, where avoiding heat becomes the main problem. Each of the first three levels has a boss; Level 4 has the molten dragon.

Murals reveal a cycle: a diver kills the sea's molten heart, founds a kingdom, and watches it freeze and sink. The kingdoms sank as they went cold, the same reason the warm diver keeps rising. At the dragon, repeat the cycle and be crowned, or preserve its warmth. Hidden rooms tell the story through earlier drowned kingdoms.

Why the idea is interesting

Movement lets you travel and defend yourself together: A kick moves you away from a creature above while directing heat toward it. Floating makes depth something you earn: every step down is a choice, and running out of heat sends you back toward safety rather than trapping you. Vents turn refueling into another movement choice, and enemy hostility gives you a reason to think about where creatures will meet.

A prototype needs to check whether enemies reliably fight each other, whether 5–10 seconds of trails keep the room readable, whether floating feels like freedom rather than a fight with the controls, and whether handholds are easy to spot at speed.

Current scope: PC, keyboard or controller; 3–4 hours, about 70 rooms, eight enemy types and four bosses. Inked characters sit on painted backgrounds. Story mode halves environmental burns, keeps heat above a quarter and widens rebound timing. This scope still needs testing.

2. How the ideas compare

To compare the ideas, we scored seven concepts and the original pitch using the same models. Higher scores are on the right. The hollow dots compare the concepts while holding execution quality constant; the filled dots also include how well each pitch claims its world, secrets and story would be made. That second score depends on those promises being met.

The deep-sea idea ranks /7 in the existing comparison, behind the moth and bird concepts. It places in the top three in of 200 reruns with resampled games. These results compare broad design traits; they do not measure how well the specific movement or enemy interactions will work.

3. The research, briefly

We described 250 popular metroidvanias and related games using 148 design traits. Movement, discovery, interacting systems and melancholy stories stood out, which suggested the ideas above. Adding those traits does not automatically make a game successful.

Two models compared traits with reviews for the 243 Steam games, accounting for release year, budget and licensed characters. We converted their outputs to a comparison scale: 50 is the average prediction when testing on games left out of training, and 10 points equals one standard deviation. The error ranges use the same conversion.

The biggest limitation is the sample: Games with few reviews are largely missing. Moreover, design ratings involve judgment, and the models do not measure marketing or polish. The results help compare concepts, but deciding which one is fun still requires building and playing it.

Sources: Steam and SteamSpy, collected 16 October 2026; console landmarks from Nintendo, VGChartz and press reports. Models: ridge regression and random forest. The original research has more detail.
